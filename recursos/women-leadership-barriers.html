The invisible barriers to women’s leadership

The problem is rarely a lack of confidence. It’s a system with unwritten rules that still decide who reaches senior leadership. This guide explains what those rules are, according to research, and how to gain influence, visibility and sponsors within it.

Level 1 · Open

Use cases

Your results are solid, but promotions come more slowly than you expected; you have good mentors, but no one championing your name in the room where decisions are made; or you feel you have to choose between being firm and being well liked.

It isn’t a confidence problem

For years, the dominant message has been that women need to “believe in themselves more”. Research points elsewhere. Robin Ely, Herminia Ibarra and Deborah Kolb called it second-generation gender bias: not open discrimination, but seemingly neutral practices and patterns (how projects are assigned, which leadership style is associated with success, who belongs to informal networks) that, taken together, favour those who already fit the dominant model.

McKinsey and LeanIn’s Women in the Workplace 2025 report adds a revealing finding: for the first time, women are less interested in being promoted than men, but that gap disappears when women receive the same career support. Ambition isn’t the problem. Support is.

Four barriers research has identified, and what you can do

Plenty of mentoring, little sponsorship

What happens: Herminia Ibarra and her colleagues found that high-potential women have as many mentors as men, or more, but fewer sponsors: people with power who use their influence to advocate for them. McKinsey’s 2025 report confirms that women still receive less sponsorship and less active advocacy from their managers.

What you can do: Distinguish who advises you from who decides. Identify the two or three people who influence your next promotion and make sure they know your ambition and have seen your work first-hand.

The double bind

What happens: Catalyst’s research describes a familiar dilemma: when a woman acts assertively she is seen as competent but less likeable; when she is warm, she is seen as likeable but less competent. Men rarely have to manage that tension.

What you can do: It isn’t about choosing one side, but about widening your influence repertoire: combining firmness and warmth deliberately, framing your proposals in terms of the other person’s goals and choosing your battles with judgment.

Work that goes unseen

What happens: Linda Babcock and her colleagues showed that women are asked, and agree, more often to take on necessary tasks that count little for promotion: organising, coordinating, training or filling gaps. They take time and are rarely recognised.

What you can do: Review where your time goes and how much of it brings you closer to senior leadership. Learn to say no, or to negotiate something in return, without damaging your reputation.

Results that don’t speak for themselves

What happens: Many women leaders trust their work to speak for them. At senior levels, however, decisions are made on reputation, and reputation is built in informal networks where access isn’t always equal.

What you can do: Work on your strategic visibility: make known not only what you’ve achieved, but how you think. A clear personal brand and a deliberate network aren’t vanity; they’re part of the job.

What also needs to change in the organisation

  1. Explicit promotion criteria. The more ambiguous what’s being assessed, the more room there is for bias.
  2. Formal sponsorship, not just mentoring. Programmes in which senior leaders commit to actively championing talent.
  3. Deliberate allocation of visible projects. Who leads strategic projects today decides who will be in senior leadership tomorrow.
  4. Accountable managers. Active support from the direct manager is one of the most influential factors in promotion.
Try this week

Your sponsor map

  1. Write down the five people with the most influence over your next promotion decision.
  2. For each, answer: do they know your ambition? Have they seen your work first-hand in the last quarter? Would they speak up for you if you weren’t in the room?
  3. Mark who is your mentor today and who could be your sponsor. They are different roles.
  4. Choose one concrete action this month with the person who has the most decision power and the least contact with you.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a mentor and a sponsor?

A mentor advises you and helps you grow. A sponsor uses their power and influence to advocate for you in the decisions that matter. Research shows women tend to have mentors but fewer sponsors, and it is sponsorship that is most associated with promotion.

What is second-generation gender bias?

It is a subtle and usually unintentional bias: seemingly neutral practices and patterns, such as project allocation or the leadership model considered successful, that together favour those who already fit the dominant profile. The concept was developed by Robin Ely, Herminia Ibarra and Deborah Kolb.

What is the double bind in women’s leadership?

It is the dilemma described by Catalyst: when a woman leads assertively she is seen as competent but less likeable; when she is warm, as likeable but less competent. Managing it means widening your influence repertoire, not choosing one of the extremes.

Does a lack of confidence hold women leaders back?

Research suggests the main factor isn’t confidence but support: according to McKinsey and LeanIn, the gap in interest in promotion between women and men disappears when women receive the same career support.
