How to prepare for an executive assessment center

An assessment center doesn’t reward the candidate who performs best, but the one who consistently shows how they lead. This guide explains what is assessed, how each exercise works and how to prepare without stopping being yourself.

Level 1 · Open

Use cases

You’ve been put forward for a senior leadership role and the process includes an assessment center; you can defend your results, but role plays and uncomfortable questions make you uneasy; or you’re wondering whether to “study” for the personality tests.

What an assessment center is

An assessment center is a structured evaluation method in which several assessors observe your behaviour across different exercises that simulate real situations in the role. It has been used since the 1950s for selection and promotion into leadership roles, and is now common in multinational companies and international institutions, often run by an external consultancy.

The logic is simple: each competency is observed in more than one exercise, and by more than one assessor. That’s why consistency matters more than isolated brilliance. A great answer in the interview that doesn’t show up in the role play doesn’t add up: it raises doubts.

What each exercise observes, and how to prepare

Competency-based interview

What is observed: Concrete examples from your career that demonstrate each competency: what you did, how, and with what result.

How to prepare: Choose two or three real situations per competency and structure them (situation, task, action, result and learning). Speak in the first person and with data, without memorising a script.

Role play

What is observed: How you handle a difficult conversation with a team member, a client or a peer: listening, influence, firmness and the ability to reach agreement.

How to prepare: The goal isn’t to “win” the conversation but to resolve it. Practise with someone who puts you on the spot, and afterwards review what you felt, not just what you said.

Business case

What is observed: Your strategic judgment: how you analyse incomplete information, prioritise, decide and present it under time pressure.

How to prepare: Practise with timed cases and train a clear structure: situation, options, recommendation, risks and next steps.

Psychometric tests

What is observed: Your personality style and sometimes your reasoning. They are cross-checked against what is observed in the other exercises.

How to prepare: Don’t try to guess the ideal profile: inconsistencies are detected. Get familiar with the format and know your own profile well.

Written self-reflection

What is observed: Your ability to analyse your career, achievements and lessons honestly and clearly.

How to prepare: Write with concrete examples and acknowledge what you learned from what didn’t go well. Make it sound like you, not a corporate report.

Recorded video interview

What is observed: Your presence and clarity on camera, with no one giving you cues.

How to prepare: Record yourself answering open questions and review it: pace, eye contact, structure and the overall impression you convey.

Reflection session

What is observed: Your self-awareness and openness to feedback when discussing the day’s results.

How to prepare: Prepare to talk naturally about your development areas and how you’re working on them. Defensiveness costs you; acknowledging with a plan adds.

The four most common mistakes

  1. Trying to guess the “ideal” profile in the tests. It usually produces results inconsistent with your behaviour in the other exercises.
  2. Bringing memorised answers. They sound scripted and don’t survive a follow-up question.
  3. Trying to win the role play. Imposing yourself isn’t leading. What is valued is the ability to build bridges between positions and reach agreement.
  4. Hiding your development areas. In the reflection session, denying the obvious signals low maturity. Acknowledging it with a plan signals the opposite.
Try this week

The evidence inventory

  1. Choose the three most important competencies for the role you’re applying for.
  2. For each, write down a real situation where you demonstrated it: what happened, what you did, what result you got and what you learned.
  3. Read them aloud and record yourself. Ask: does this sound like me, or like what I think they want to hear?
  4. Ask someone you trust to ask two follow-up questions about each story. Wherever you hesitate, there’s work to do.

How much time you need to prepare

Ideally, start around six months ahead, especially if you’re preparing for your next step without a fixed date: it gives time for in-depth work on your presence, emotional management and development areas. If the process has already been announced, eight weeks is a reasonable minimum for intensive preparation with real practice.

Frequently asked questions

Can you prepare for the psychometric tests in an assessment center?

You can get familiar with the format and know your profile well, but it isn’t wise to answer the way you think they expect: results are cross-checked against your behaviour in the other exercises, and inconsistencies count against you.

What matters most in an assessment center?

Consistency. Each competency is observed across several exercises and by several assessors, so consistently showing how you lead matters more than having perfect answers in any single exercise.

How do you prepare for an assessment center role play?

By practising difficult conversations with someone who puts you on the spot, then analysing both what you said and what you felt. The goal is to resolve the situation and reach agreement, not to impose yourself.

How far in advance should you prepare?

Ideally around six months. If the process has already been announced, a minimum of eight weeks allows intensive preparation with real practice.
